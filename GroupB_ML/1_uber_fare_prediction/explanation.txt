ASSIGNMENT 1 — UBER RIDE FARE PREDICTION
========================================

Problem Statement
-----------------

Predict the price of the Uber ride from a given pickup point to the agreed drop-off location.
1. Pre-process the dataset.
2. Identify outliers.
3. Check the correlation.
4. Implement linear regression and random forest regression models. Evaluate the models and compare their respective scores like R2, RMSE, etc.

Dataset: Uber Fares Dataset (Kaggle) (https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/yasserh/uber-fares-dataset)

Explanation
-----------

1. Pre-processing (preprocess)
- Drops rows with missing values.
- Filters out invalid latitude/longitude values (outside -180 to 180).
- Computes distance_km between pickup and drop-off coordinates using the Haversine formula (great-circle distance on a sphere — accounts for the Earth's curvature, unlike simple Euclidean distance on lat/long).
- Removes rows with non-positive fare or distance.

2. Outlier detection (remove_outliers_iqr)
Uses the IQR (Interquartile Range) method: compute Q1 (25th percentile) and Q3 (75th percentile), then anything below Q1 - 1.5×IQR or above Q3 + 1.5×IQR is flagged as an outlier and removed. Applied to both fare_amount and distance_km. Box plots are drawn before/after to visualize the effect.

3. Correlation check
A correlation matrix is computed between fare_amount, distance_km, and passenger_count, visualized as a heatmap. distance_km should show a strong positive correlation with fare_amount (makes physical sense — longer rides cost more), while passenger_count typically shows weak correlation.

4. Model training & comparison
- Linear Regression — fits a straight-line relationship between features (distance_km, passenger_count) and the target (fare_amount).
- Random Forest Regression — an ensemble of decision trees; each tree is trained on a random subset of data/features, and predictions are averaged, capturing non-linear relationships that linear regression cannot.
- Both are evaluated using R² (how much variance in fare is explained by the model; closer to 1 is better) and RMSE (Root Mean Squared Error — average prediction error in the same units as fare; lower is better).

Viva Questions & Answers
------------------------

Q1. Why use the Haversine formula instead of simple Euclidean distance for lat/long coordinates?
Latitude/longitude are coordinates on a sphere, not a flat plane — Euclidean distance on raw degrees doesn't correspond to actual physical distance. Haversine accounts for the Earth's curvature to give accurate real-world distance in kilometers.

Q2. What is the IQR method and why is it used for outlier detection?
It defines "normal" values as those within [Q1 - 1.5×IQR, Q3 + 1.5×IQR], where IQR = Q3 - Q1. It's robust to extreme values (unlike mean/standard-deviation-based methods) because it's based on percentiles, making it a standard, simple way to flag anomalies like a $500 fare for a 1 km ride.

Q3. What does a correlation coefficient close to 1 between distance and fare mean?
It means the two variables move together strongly and linearly — as distance increases, fare increases proportionally, which is expected for ride pricing.

Q4. Why use both Linear Regression and Random Forest instead of just one model?
To compare a simple, interpretable linear model against a more flexible, non-linear ensemble model — this shows whether the fare-distance relationship is adequately linear or whether a more complex model captures patterns better (reflected in the R²/RMSE comparison).

Q5. What do R² and RMSE each tell you, and how do you interpret them?
R² (0 to 1) indicates the proportion of variance in the target explained by the model — higher is better. RMSE measures average prediction error in the target's own units (dollars, here) — lower is better. A model can have decent R² but still have a practically large RMSE depending on the scale of the data.

Q6. Why might Random Forest sometimes score worse than Linear Regression here?
If the true relationship between distance and fare is close to linear (as pricing usually is — roughly $X per km plus a base fare), a Random Forest can overfit to noise in the training data and generalize slightly worse than a simple linear model, especially with limited features.

Q7. Why split the data into train and test sets instead of evaluating on all the data?
Evaluating on data the model was trained on would overestimate performance (the model has already "seen" the answers) — a held-out test set measures how well the model generalizes to new, unseen rides.

Q8. What would happen if outliers were not removed before training?
Extreme values (e.g. a wildly mispriced fare) would disproportionately pull the regression line/trees toward fitting those anomalies, degrading prediction accuracy on normal, typical rides.

Q9. Why is passenger_count only weakly correlated with fare?
Fare is primarily driven by trip distance (and time/surge pricing), not how many passengers are in the car — Uber doesn't charge significantly more per additional passenger for a standard ride.

Q10. What additional features could improve this model in a real-world setting?
Time of day / day of week (traffic, surge pricing), weather conditions, actual route distance (vs straight-line distance), and pickup/drop-off zone/borough.
